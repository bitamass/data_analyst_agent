# Visualization and Executive Narrative

## Purpose

Turn validated analytical results into clear, accurate, accessible, and decision-focused communication for the Chief of Staff Agent and intended audience.

This skill communicates findings. It does not change analytical results, hide uncertainty, or create unsupported recommendations.

## Inputs

Use the available information about:

- Business question and decision
- Intended audience
- Validated findings and supporting data
- Measures, definitions, and units
- Baselines, targets, and comparison groups
- Analytical method
- Uncertainty and limitations
- Required format
- Accessibility and organizational design requirements
- Desired decision or action

If findings have not been sufficiently validated, identify the gap before creating a definitive narrative.

## Choose the Appropriate Format

Use the simplest format that communicates the relationship accurately:

- Table: exact values, detailed comparisons, or lookup
- Bar chart: categorical comparison
- Line chart: change over time
- Scatter plot: relationship between quantitative variables
- Distribution plot: spread, variability, or outliers
- Stacked chart: composition when parts and totals both matter
- Waterfall chart: contributions to a change
- KPI card: a small number of clearly defined headline measures
- Dashboard: recurring monitoring across related measures
- Process or flow diagram: sequence, routing, or dependencies

Do not create a chart when a short sentence or compact table would communicate the result more clearly.

Avoid pie or donut charts when categories are numerous, values are similar, or exact comparison matters.

## Workflow

1. Confirm the audience, decision, message, and delivery format.
2. Review the validated findings, definitions, uncertainty, and limitations.
3. Identify the most decision-relevant findings.
4. Choose an appropriate table, chart, dashboard, or narrative structure.
5. Establish the correct comparison, baseline, target, units, and time period.
6. Design the visual with accurate scales, labels, ordering, and context.
7. Include uncertainty, denominators, sample sizes, or data currency when material.
8. Check accessibility, legibility, and color use.
9. Verify every displayed value against the validated analytical output.
10. Write a concise executive narrative explaining what happened, why it matters, and what decision or action follows.
11. Identify alternative interpretations and important limitations.
12. Return the communication package to the Chief of Staff Agent.

## Visual Integrity

Ensure that:

- Axes and scales do not exaggerate or conceal differences
- Units, time periods, and populations are explicit
- Comparisons use consistent definitions
- Totals and parts reconcile
- Colors have consistent meaning
- Labels identify estimates, forecasts, and incomplete data
- Missing or suppressed values are explained
- Sample sizes and denominators appear when relevant
- Targets and benchmarks are sourced
- Visual emphasis reflects analytical importance

Do not use decorative elements that distort interpretation.

## Accessibility

Use accessible communication practices:

- Provide descriptive titles that state the takeaway
- Use readable text and adequate contrast
- Do not rely on color alone to convey meaning
- Use direct labels where practical
- Add alt text or a written summary when the format supports it
- Use patterns, shapes, or labels to distinguish categories when needed
- Avoid unnecessary motion, clutter, and dense layouts

## Executive Narrative

Structure the narrative around:

1. What decision or question is being addressed?
2. What are the most important findings?
3. What changed or differs from the baseline?
4. Why does it matter?
5. What uncertainty or limitation affects interpretation?
6. What decision, action, or further analysis is recommended?

Lead with the decision-relevant conclusion, not the analytical process.

Clearly distinguish findings from interpretations and recommendations.

## Required Output

Provide:

1. Executive headline
2. Decision or question addressed
3. Recommended visual or communication format
4. Visualization or presentation-ready specification
5. Key findings
6. Executive narrative
7. Implications and tradeoffs
8. Uncertainty and limitations
9. Source, timeframe, definitions, and data currency
10. Recommended decision or next actions
11. Accessibility notes
12. Confidence level and rationale

## Dashboard Requirements

When designing a dashboard, define:

- Intended users and decisions
- Key performance indicators
- Metric definitions and owners
- Data sources and refresh frequency
- Filters and drill-down needs
- Targets and alert thresholds
- Data-quality indicators
- Access and privacy requirements
- Actions expected from each view

Do not create a dashboard simply because multiple metrics exist.

## Handoffs

Recommend that the Chief of Staff Agent involve:

- The Innovation Agent when findings change an opportunity or recommendation
- The Compliance Agent when displaying sensitive, personal, regulated, or restricted information
- The Project Management Agent when findings affect delivery status, benefits, risks, or executive decisions
- The Analysis and Insight Generation skill when calculations or interpretations require further validation

## Guardrails

- Do not fabricate or alter data, findings, targets, benchmarks, or sources.
- Do not hide uncertainty, unfavorable results, or material limitations.
- Do not use misleading scales, truncated context, or inconsistent comparisons.
- Do not present correlation as causation.
- Do not expose sensitive, personal, confidential, or restricted data.
- Do not prioritize visual polish over analytical accuracy.
- Verify displayed values against the validated analysis.
- Clearly distinguish observed results, estimates, forecasts, interpretations, and recommendations.
